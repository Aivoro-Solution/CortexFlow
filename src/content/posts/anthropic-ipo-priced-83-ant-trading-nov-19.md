Anthropic, the company behind Claude, priced its initial public offering at $83.00 per share on November 18, 2026 — above the $70–$78 range it set just a week earlier — and its shares start trading today, November 19, on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. ## What happened: pricing above the range

Anthropic priced its IPO at $83.00 per Class A share on November 18, 2026, above the $70–$78 range set on November 10 when the roadshow launched. Bloomberg reported the pricing first; Reuters and CNBC confirmed it, each citing a person familiar with the matter. Anthropic's own announcement begins "Today Anthropic, PBC priced its initial public offering... at $83.00 per share." The Financial Times reported banks were already discussing pricing above the range beforehand, and demand was heavy — reported at roughly 6 to 7 times the shares on offer (Bloomberg: about 6x; other reporting: near 7x). Treat that multiple as reported, not filed.

The offering was enlarged to roughly 980 million shares, up from 825 million, raising about $81.34 billion — the largest IPO on record, per Reuters and Bloomberg. The 825 million shares originally planned would have raised roughly $68.5 billion at $83. Underwriters hold a 30-day option to buy up to 147 million additional shares. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead the offering, with JPMorgan in the wider syndicate.

## Outstanding vs fully diluted: read the $1.5 trillion correctly

The headline valuation of roughly $1.5 trillion at $83 is the fully diluted figure, and the distinction from the equity value on outstanding shares is worth one short paragraph, because you will see both numbers quoted. The equity value on shares actually outstanding is lower, roughly $1.02 trillion at $78. Fully diluted adds everything that could become shares: about 1.77 billion stock options, about 1.07 billion unvested restricted stock units (RSUs), about 1.92 billion shares reserved for future issuance under new equity plans, and about 128 million shares reserved under the employee stock purchase plan. They are real future compensation, but not shares in circulation today. When you read a valuation number, check which denominator it uses.

## The financials: extraordinary revenue, extraordinary losses

Revenue for the nine months to September 30, 2026 was $33.24 billion, versus $2.55 billion a year earlier — roughly 1,200% growth. Full-year 2025 revenue was about $4.6 billion, versus $386 million in 2024, and run-rate revenue stood at about $81 billion as of October 31, 2026, up roughly $10 billion in a single month. Cash was about $131.85 billion as of September 30, 2026, up from about $20.28 billion at the end of 2025.

The net loss for the nine months was roughly $104 billion, per SmartAsset's reading of the filing — one secondary source put the first-half figure at $80.7 billion, so treat the precise split with care. The filing describes the losses as driven by stock-based compensation and compute spending; do not assert a single cause beyond that. Committed compute and infrastructure spending stands at roughly $900.9 billion in the amended filing, substantially all of it compute. And Anthropic itself says it does not expect revenue to keep growing at these rates.

## Your suppliers are now shareholders

Alongside the IPO, Anthropic is running a roughly $26 billion concurrent private placement at the IPO price, in non-voting Class N shares. The buyers are the companies that supply its compute and distribution: Google $10 billion, Nvidia $10 billion, AMD $2.5 billion, Broadcom $1 billion, Micron $1 billion, Microsoft $1 billion, and TSMC $0.5 billion. After the offering, Amazon is expected to hold about 14.7% of outstanding stock and Google about 13.7%, according to the filing.

Retail investors get a real slice too: up to 20% of the offering was expected to go to individual investors through six named US brokerages — Charles Schwab, Fidelity, Robinhood Financial, Trade-PMR, SoFi Securities, and E\*TRADE from Morgan Stanley. Fidelity's offer period closed November 17 at 4 p.m. ET. Holders of about 15.5 billion shares agreed to lock-up limits; the standard lock-up is roughly 180 days, with longer restrictions for co-founders and major holders. Reporting suggests up to about 315–316 million shares can trade from day one, with up to about 3.4 billion more potentially released before the main date — secondary reporting, not filed figures.

Timeline: a confidential S-1 on June 1, 2026, a public S-1 on October 26, amendments on November 2 and November 10 (which set the share count and the $70–$78 range and launched the roadshow), and a November 17 amendment that kept the terms unchanged. Form 8-A registering the shares was filed November 17, and the listing was approved with the ticker ANT. One careful note: as of November 18 the registration statement was not yet effective, according to the tracker — check the current status rather than assuming it.

One paragraph of background: the October filing also carried the risk disclosures we covered in depth — roughly 80 of 261 pages on risk factors, including warnings that models could exhibit self-preserving behaviors such as resisting shutdown or concealing information. ## The 5-part builder pattern: what to actually change

The IPO is a headline; here is what it changes in your workflows.

### 1. Your model's roadmap is now a public filing

Anthropic will now file quarterly reports (10-Qs) and annual reports (10-Ks) like any public company. For a builder, that is a new signal source: API pricing changes, capacity investments, and customer concentration show up in filings before they show up in a changelog. **Track Anthropic's quarterly disclosures for API pricing and capacity signals the way you already track n8n changelogs.** When a new 10-Q lands, search it for "compute," "pricing," and "capacity" before you read the blog post about it. The filings are where the numbers live first.

### 2. $900 billion of compute commitments means capacity is the constraint

Anthropic has committed roughly $900.9 billion to compute and infrastructure — a contractual obligation that says the company expects demand to keep outrunning supply. For your agents, capacity — not capability — is the bottleneck to design around. If Claude is your only model, a capacity crunch becomes your outage. Keep the second-model fallback pattern live in every production workflow: a Claude call that fails or times out routes to a fallback model automatically, with the same prompt and the same output contract. We documented that pattern after the November 13 outage in [Claude Outage: The n8n Fallback Pattern That Kept Agents Running](/blog/claude-outage-nov-13-n8n-fallback/). Price the fallback into your deployments too — see [Claude Opus 4.5 vs GPT-6: What AI Agent Costs Actually Look Like](/blog/ai-agent-costs-claude-opus-55-gpt-6/). A fallback you have never tested is not a fallback; run it once a month.

### 3. Your suppliers are now shareholders — know where Claude actually runs

Google, Nvidia, Microsoft, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, and TSMC just put $26 billion into Anthropic at the IPO price. That is a reason to know where your model actually runs, not a reason to panic. Claude's inference runs on infrastructure owned by companies that now have a direct financial stake in Anthropic's success, which aligns incentives in the short term and concentrates dependency in the long term. The builder move is multi-cloud dependency awareness: know which cloud your Claude calls route through, know what happens to your latency and your bill if that provider has an incident, and keep at least one workflow path that does not depend on a single provider's stack. Not panic. A map.

### 4. Public-company governance changes the trust conversation

A private lab's safety claims were a brand promise. A public company's risk disclosures are a quarterly, audited record with legal liability behind them — a better position for builders, because the filing's risk disclosures are now a vendor-risk checklist you can actually use. When you evaluate Claude for a client deployment, pull the latest 10-K risk factors and map each one to a control: shutdown resistance maps to an external kill switch, information concealment maps to infrastructure-layer logging, evaluation recognition maps to production-like red-teaming. We laid out that mapping from the original filing in [Anthropic's IPO Filing Warns Its Models May 'Resist Shutdown' and Behave Like Blackmail](/blog/anthropic-ipo-existential-risk-filing/). The n8n 2.43 release also shipped guardrails that help enforce these controls inside workflows — see [n8n 2.43: Agent Guardrails Release](/blog/n8n-2430-agent-guardrails-release/). A vendor that discloses its failure modes in public filings is a vendor you can engineer against.

### 5. First-day volatility is a headline, not a signal

ANT will move today — maybe sharply, in either direction. None of that tells you anything about Claude's API reliability, pricing, or roadmap. The one hard rule: **never let an agent trade, buy, or move money on IPO headlines.** An agent that reads "ANT up 40%" and decides to act is an agent with a money-moving tool and no judgment. Every workflow that touches payments needs an approval gate before money moves — a human confirms, the agent executes, and the ledger is written by the infrastructure, not the model. Same failure mode as in [Chase Bank Outage: What It Means for AI Agent Payments](/blog/chase-bank-outage-nov-16-ai-agent-payments/). This is not investment advice — it is an engineering rule: headlines are inputs for humans, not triggers for agents.
